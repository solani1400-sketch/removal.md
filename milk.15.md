Abstract
In the present work, the synthesis and characterisation of a molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) by precipitation polymerisation
using tetracycline (TC) as a template molecule, methacrylic acid as a functional monomer, ethylene glycol dimethacrylate as a
cross-linker, persulphate as an initiator, and methanol as a porogen solvent is described. The molecular recognition properties and
selectivity of MIPs against four TCs [(TC), oxytetracycline (OT), chlortetracycline (CT), and doxycycline (DT)] were evaluated,
and the results demonstrated high selectivity for four TCs in milk samples without sample treatment. Under the optimal synthesis
conditions (TC/methacrylic acid/ethylene glycol dimethacrylate ratio of 1.0:7.0:50.0), the percent removal obtained was 81.83%
for TC, 95.47% for OT, 96.44% for CT, and 93.25% for DT using 1.0 mL of the sample, 20 mg of the sorbent, a pH of 6.0, and
15 min of contact time. One of the tested samples was positive for the presence of OT, with a concentration of 19.83 μg L
−1
,
ensuring the complete removal of TC using the proposed method. The method provided significant data regarding the develop-
ment of efficient and selective materials in the removal of several residues and contaminants.

Introduction
Tetracyclines (TCs) are a family of antibiotics used in human
and veterinary medicine, and they are considered broad spec-
trum bacteriostatic agents [1]. Currently, there are over 20 TCs
available; however, in recent decades, TC, chlortetracycline
(CT), oxytetracycline (OT), and doxycycline (DT) have been
the most commonly used (Fig. 1). Due to their activity against
both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, this family of
antibiotics has been used in the prevention and treatment of
infectious diseases. In the livestock industry, the use of TCs
has increased in recent decades since they are employed as
feed additives in sub-therapeutic doses to promote rapid
growth and weight gain [2–4].
The indiscriminate use of TCs causes their incomplete
metabolisation or elimination by excretion [5–8].The presence
of TC residues in foods of animal origin can have several ef-
fects on human health, such as allergic reactions, liver damage,
teeth damage, gastrointestinal disturbances, antibiotic resistant,
and transgenerational effects [9–11]. In dairy cows, significant
percentages of the administered TCs are excreted in milk. To
protect human health, regulation authorities have established
tolerance levels in milk [12]. The United States Food and
Drug Administration [13] has set 300 μg kg
−1 
(TC, OT, and
CT combined) as the maximum residue limit (MRL), and the
European Union [14] has set 100 μg kg
−1 
(TC, OT, and CT
combined) as the MRL. For countries where TCs are not reg-
ulated, the Codex Alimentarius was adopted, with a limit of
199 μg kg
−1 
(sum of the TC, OT, and CT residues combined or
each individual TC) [15].
Due to the problems caused by antibiotics residues, several
removal treatments, such as the use of composite polymer elec-
trolytes (CPEs) [16], magnetic ion-exchange resin (MIEX®)
[17], carbonaceous materials [18], sludge-derived adsorbents
[19], and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) [20], have been

In recent years, molecular recognition has received consid-
erable attention as a promising approach for the removal of
antibiotics residues in complex matrices due to its specific and
selective recognition cavities. Molecularly imprinted poly-
mers (MIPs) use a target molecule (template) during the syn-
thesis process in the presence of functional monomers in order
to obtain covalent or noncovalent interactions [21]. The pro-
cess involves a copolymerisation reaction between the func-
tional monomer, the template, and the cross-linked agent.
After the reaction, the template molecule is removed from
the MIP before use [22–24].
According to the binding sites, the MIPs provide high selec-
tivity for the analytes through their recognition cavities through
hydrogen bonding, ion exchange, and electrostatic interactions.
Therefore, many MIPs have been applied in solid-phase extrac-
tion (SPE), solid-phase micro-extraction (SPME) [25], dispersive
solid-phase extraction (DSPE), and magnetic solid-phase extrac-
tion (MSPE) [26, 27], with separation and removal of metal ions
[28], organic acid compounds [29], pesticides [30, 31], and chiral
compounds [25, 32] in complex matrices, such biological, food,
and environmental samples [33–35].
The present work describes a new method for the removal
of TC residues in milk samples based on the use of MIPs
under dispersive solid-phase micro-extraction (DSPME).
The adsorbent materials were synthesised using the concept
of molecular recognition. According to the nature of the ad-
sorbent, the template proportion, functional monomer, cross-
linking molar ratio, and porogen solvent were modified during
the polymerisation process to evaluate the removal efficiency
under different experimental conditions, varying the pH,
amount of MIP, contact time, ionic strength, and initial TC
concentration, analysed by large volume sample stacking cap-
illary electrophoresis (LVSS-CE).
Experimental
Chemicals and materials
Tetracycline hydrochloride, chlortetracycline hydrochloride,
oxytetracycline dehydrate, doxycycline hydrate, methacrylic
acid (MAA), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA), so-
dium persulfate, sodium phosphate, and picric acid were ob-
tained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).
Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), sodium hydroxide
(NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), methanol (MeOH), and
acetonitrile (ACN) were obtained from J.T. Baker
(Phillipsburg, NJ USA), and 2-propanol was obtained from
Fluka (St. Gallen, Switzerland). All solutions were prepared
by dissolving the respective reagent in deionised water, with a
resistivity not less than 18.2 MΩcm, provided by a Milli-Q
system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). The electrolyte solu-

Synthesis of the molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)
The MIPs were synthesised according the following sequence
(Fig. 2). First, the MIPs were obtained by precipitation poly-
merisation, and the pre-assembly solution was carried out with
the addition of 0.1 mmol of TC (template molecule) and
0.7 mmol of MAA (functional monomer) in 30 mL of meth-
anol (porogen solvent). The mixture was vigorously stirred for
120 min [36]. Afterwards, the previously prepared pre-
assembly solution was mixed with 4.9 mmol of EGDMA
(cross-linker agent), and 80 mg of sodium persulfate (initiator
agent) was added for preparation of the pre-polymerisation
solution. Once the initiator agent was added, the mixture
was purged with nitrogen gas for 15 min in order to ensure
that the solution was oxygen free. The polymerisation process
was carried out by refluxing at 60 °C for 16 h, with stirring.
Once the polymerisation process concluded, the polymers
were separated by centrifugation (2500 rpm, 5 min) and
washed with methanol/acetic acid (9:1, v/v) with a Soxhlet
extractor to remove the template molecule and the unreacted
reagents. Finally, the MIP was dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The
non-imprinted polymers (NIPs) were synthesised and proc-
essed in a way similar to that described above, except the
template molecule was not added.
Apparatus
Infrared characterisation of the adsorbents was performed
with a Perkin Elmer Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spec-
trophotometer with a Pike Gladi ATR. Morphological analy-
sis of the adsorbents was performed using a JEOL JSM-820
scanning electron microscope (SEM).
Electrophoresis was performed using a Beckman Coulter
P/ACE 5500 (Fullerton, CA, USA) photodiode array detector.
Data were analysed with a Beckman P/ACE system with
MDQ version 2.3 software. TC separations were performed
in a fused silica capillary (41.7 cm × 75 μm ID). A pH/ion
analyser (model 450; Corning Science Products, NY, USA)
was used to accurately adjust the pH of the electrolyte solution
to within 0.01 pH units. A Branson Ultrasonic system
(Danbury, CT, USA), model 3510, was used in the dispersion
of the MIPs during TC removal of the sample matrix. Finally,
a Maxi-Mix I (Barnstead/Thermolyne, IA, USA) was used as
a vortex mixer.
Application of MIP by DSPME for tetracycline (TC) re-
moval in milk samples
The DSPME procedure is shown in Fig. 3. First, 20 mg of
MIPs were added to an Eppendorf tube and conditioned with
1.0 mL of water (three times) in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min.
The MIPs were then isolated by centrifugation (10,000 rpm,
5 min), and the supernatant was discarded. During the remov-
al process, 1.0 mL of the milk sample was added to the
Eppendorf tube, and the mixture was stirred by sonication
for 15 min. After the removal process, the MIPs with the
retained analytes were isolated from the sample matrix by
centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 5 min), and the supernatant (milk
samples) was treated for subsequent analysis by LVSS-CE.
Sample treatment
After the removal process, the supernatant (milk sample) was
spiked with picric acid as an internal standard (IS), and pro-
teins were precipitated by adding 0.2 mL of 2% acetic acid,
followed by heating for 10 min (60 °C) in a water bath and
centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently the
protein-free liquid phase was diluted to 10 mL with
deionised water. The solution was then passed through a
cartridge (Discovery® DSC-18 cartridges, 500 mg) that
was previously activated with 5 mL of MeOH and 5 mL of
deionised water at a flow rate of 1 mL min
−1
. The analytes
that were retained on the cartridge were washed with 2 mL of
5% MeOH. The retained TCs were eluted with 3 mL of
MeOH, and the eluted solution was evaporated to dryness

Results and discussion
Chemical and morphological characterisation of the
synthesised polymers
The degree of efficiency during the removal of TCs depended
on the composition of the MIP. For this reason, seven MIPs
were synthesised and characterised. The MIPs were obtained
at the following different molar ratios of the functional mono-

1.0:7.0:25.0, 1.0:5.0:50.0, 1.0:6.0:50.0, 1.0:7.0:50.0,
1.0:8.0:50.0, 1.0:9.0:50.0 and 1.0:7.0:75.0 (template/MAA/
EDGMA). The synthesised MIPs were evaluated under the
DSPME modality in the removal of TCs. To identify the func-
tional groups presents on the MIPs and to confirm the poly-
meric reaction, FTIR spectroscopy studies were performed

Effect of the polarity of the porogenic solvent on the
removal of TCs
In order to evaluate the effect of the polarity of the porogenic
solvent in the pre-polymerisation complex (template/function-
al monomer) and as a consequence of the removal of TCs. The
adequate selection of the porogenic solvent can ensure the
formation of a microporous polymer or a macroporous poly-
mer. The polarity was evaluated using ACN and MeOH as
aprotic and protic solvents, respectively, during the synthesis
process. The synthesis of the MIP was performed at different
ratios of MeOH to ACN (4:0, 3:1, 2:2, 1:3, and 0:4) in a final
volume of 30 mL. According to the obtained results (Fig. 6),
the solvent polarity significantly affects the interaction mode
(template/functional monomer). The highest TC removal per-
centages were obtained using MeOH and ACN in a ratio of
4:0 (RSD <5% in all cases, n = 3); however, if the polarity of
the solvent decreases, the TC percent of removal decreases
until affecting the removal of all the TCs, indicating the rela-
tionship between the imprinting factors of the obtained MIPs
and the dielectric constant of the porogenic solvents [41]. In
addition, when the MeOH to ACN ratio was 0:1, the forma-
tion of a solid was not observed in the polymerisation system.
For this reason, MeOH was used in order to obtain the best
removal conditions.

the TCS in their neutral state.
Adsorption analysis and recognition mechanism of
the MIPs
Selection of an adequate amount of the MIP is necessary to
ensure complete removal of the target analytes. The effect of
the amount of the MIP on the removal of TC, CT, OT, and DT
was determined by employing 5.0–30.0 mg of MIP in the sample
treatment. Figure 8a shows the maximum TC removal percent-
age using 20.0 mg of the MIP. In addition, the effect of the
contact time on the removal capacity of the four TCs on the
MIP and the NIP was evaluated from 5.0 to 60.0 min using
20 mg of the MIP and the NIP. Figure 8b shows the mean TC
removal percentage for the MIP and the NIP. Based on the ob-
tained results, the percent removal increased with time, reaching
binding equilibrium on the MIP and the NIP in 15 min, with TC
percent removals of 92.63 and 15.24%, respectively (RSD <5%
in all cases, n = 3). The obtained results indicate different types of
interactions and, therefore, different removal mechanisms. As a
consequence, 20 mg of the MIP and a contact time of 15 min
were used for subsequent experiments.

Selectivity analysis
The selectively of the MIP during TC removal was evaluated
in the presence of the following three antibiotics: ciprofloxa-
cin (CIP), norfloxacin (NOR), and sulfamethoxasol (SUL),
which are veterinary residues found in milk samples [46,
47]. Each experiment was carried out in the presence of each
antibiotic at three different molar ratio levels (TC/antibiotic)
(0.5:1.0, 1.0:1.0, 1.0:2.0, 1.0:3.0, and 1.0:4.0). Figure 9 shows
the comparative electropherograms. According to the obtain-
ed results, the percent removal was not affected by any con-
centration level of CIP, NOR, or SUL, and the percent remov-
al of each TC was from 82 to 96%, with a % RSD of <5% in
all cases (n = 3).

Reusability
The reusability and stability of the MIP adsorbent are
important advantages over other materials with a single-
use, allowing obtains process economics. To demon-
strate the reusability and stability of the MIP particles
was used 20 mg of MIP on a successive process on the
removal of TCs from milk samples. According to the
results obtained, the percent removal slightly decreased

Conclusion
The proposed technique based on the use of MIPs, synthesised
using TC as a template molecule, MAA as a functional mono-
mer, and EGDMA as a crosslinker agent (with a molar ratio of
1.0:7.0:50.0), in the removal of TC in milk samples was dem-
onstrated to be an efficient and highly selective strategy in the
removal of four TCs (TC, CT, OT, and DT) in complex ma-
trices such milk. The synthesised MIPs, which were applied
with DSPME, were faster than the classical removal process,
with minimum sample manipulation, lower solvent consump-
tion, and, consequently, lower cost, with a percent removal of
81.83–96.44% and a % RSD of <5% in all cases.
