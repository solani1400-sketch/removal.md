A B S T R A C T
Water purification has always being an imperative but challenging issue of our times. Here, we used an organic-
inorganic material, i.e. bacterial cellulose (BC) and Ca-montmorillonite (Ca-MMT) composites to treat complex
wastewater. The surface and inside of the BC/Ca-MMT was a microporous structure capable of providing
abundant adsorption sites. We demonstrated the BC/Ca-MMT has superior removal efficiency towards methy-
lene blue (MB) and tetracycline (TC). Typically, the sample showed significant uptake ability towards MB and TC
with uptake characteristics of pseudo-second-order model and Langmuir isotherm model. More interestingly, in
MB-TC binary system, the removal of the two contaminative species was hardly affected by other coexisting
components. Meanwhile, the sample was ease of regeneration and kept stable reusability through consecutive
four recycles. With more virtues, such as low cost and wide range of resources of the two raw materials, the
BC/Ca-MMT is expected to be a promising versatile water purifier in sewage treatment.
1. Introduction
With the development of progressive industrialization and urbani-
zation, the increasing lack of clean and safe water is among the most
pressing global challenges nowadays (Werber, Osuji, & Elimelech,
2016). A series of severe contamination of water resources issues are
being brought about by the discharge of industrial effluents containing
highly toxic organic and inorganic pollutants. Dyes from textile and
dyestuff industries have been considered to be one of the main com-
ponent of sewage. (Jiang et al., 2019). They have a negative impact on
the environment and even economic, because of the reactive toxic in-
gredients of them are usually teratogenic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic.
However, its complex and stable structures make it difficult and slow to
degradation in nature (Fu & Viraraghavan, 2001). Extensively used
antibiotics have been proved to be a class of potent pollutants of
emerging concern with a variety of potential adverse effects (Michael
et al., 2013). Although the benefits of antibiotics in therapy, animal
farming or agricultural are undisputed (Martinez, 2009). Unfortunately,
excessive use of them will cause serious impact on aquatic photo-
synthetic organisms and microbial populations, as well as the dis-
semination into antibiotic-resistant genes among microorganisms
(Aristilde, Marichal, Miehe-Brendle, Lanson, & Charlet, 2010). Conse-
quently, not only species living in water but also the broader biological
community are being affected due to the chronic or acute toxicity of
dyes and antibiotics (Guo et al., 2015).
So far, the demand for water purification technology with sustain-
able, powerful and energy efficient is still urgent (Zhang et al., 2013).
Adsorption is one of the most recognized approaches with advantages
of high feasibility, high efficiency, fewer by-products and easy opera-
tion (Blackburn, 2004). However, the need for expensive precursors
and complex procedures involved in the synthesis of adsorbents dra-
matically hamper their actual applications. Thus, more economical and
cheaper alternative adsorbents are still developing (Olivera et al.,
2016). Currently, various adsorbents, such as carbon nanotubes (Anjum
et al., 2019), cellulose (Abouzeid, Khiari, El-Wakil, & Dufresne, 2019;
Bethke et al., 2018), metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) (Jiang et al.,
2019; Kobielska, Howarth, Farha, & Nayak, 2018), clay (Yadav, Gadi, &
Kalra, 2019), graphene (Li, Liu, Zeng, Liu, & Liu, 2019), and chitosan
(Pakdel & Peighambardoust, 2018) have been applied for wastewater
treatment.
Bacterial cellulose (BC) is a type of natural polysaccharides mainly
produced by Acetobacter xylinum (Cheng, Yang, Liu, Liu, & Chen, 2017),
a green biomass regarded as one of the most promising bio-derived
nanomaterials. Our group is also interested in exploring its applications
in many fields such as energy storage, photocatalysis, biomedical, and
water treatment (Chen et al., 2016, 2018; Hu et al., 2019; Huang et al.,
2015, 2016; Yang et al., 2011), due to its unique three-dimensional
(3D) nanostructure, large surface area, excellent biocompatibility, high

excellent reusability. In a sense, this work will have profound influence
on wider application of organic-inorganic composites as adsorbents.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
BC hydrogels were produced by using Acetobacter xylinum NUST4.2
stocked in our laboratory through a static fermentation process at 30 °C
for 72 h, the cultural conditions described in a previous work (Zhou,
Sun, Hu, Li, & Yang, 2007). Briefly, They were purified with 0.1 M
sodium hydroxide solution at 80 °C for 3 h and then washed with dis-
tilled water several times (Yang et al., 2009). The raw Ca-MMT was
obtained from Wancheng Bentonite Co. Ltd. (Heishang, Liaoning,
China). The value of cation exchange capacity (CEC) of Ca-MMT mea-
sured by ammonium chloride-ethanol exchange was 90.8 mmol/100 g.
Sodium hydroxide (97%), methyl blue, tetracycline hydrochloride
(99%), humic acid, sodium alginate were purchased from Shanghai
Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid and ethanol were pur-
chased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, and both were of
analytic grade.
2.2. Preparation of BC/Ca-MMT composites
The raw Ca-MMT was dried in oven over night at 60 °C and then
ground to a size less than 300 mesh. Subsequently, a 2% Ca-MMT
suspension was prepared by dispersing the refined material into dis-
tilled water, which was kept continuously stirring for 2 h to obtain
homogenous mixtures with the smallest possible particle size. BC hy-
drogel (moisture content = 99.5%) was sliced into the customized
shape (20 mm × 20 mm) and then immersed in the Ca-MMT suspen-
sions. The mixtures were taken place in a conical flask with stirring at
150 rpm, 30 °C for 24 h (Ul-Islam et al., 2012). The final BC/Ca-MMT
composites were gained after washing with distilled water several times
to remove residuals. Lastly, a standard freezing-dried procedure at
−54 °C for 24 h was applied to achieve the sponge-like product samples
(Scheme 1), which were stored in the drier before further use.

2.4. Adsorption experiments
The adsorption isotherm was investigated with the initial MB and
TC concentrations of 20–170 mg/L using batch adsorption experiments.
The effect of pH was evaluated at an initial concentration of 50 mg/L in
the pH range of 2–10. The experiments were carried out by mixing
10 mg BC/Ca-MMT with 20 ml MB or TC aqueous solutions shaken at
150 rpm, 30 °C for 5 h. After reaching adsorption equilibrium, the
suspensions were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and the concentra-
tion of MB or TC in the solution was measured by an UV-1800 spec-
trophotometer (AOE instruments, China) at 640 nm or 356 nm, re-
spectively.
The kinetic adsorption experiment was investigated with the initial
MB and TC concentrations of 70 mg/L. Typically, 40 mg BC/Ca-MMT
were added into 100 mL MB or TC aqueous solutions and then shook at
150 rpm, 30 °C, respectively. At each predetermined time interval, a
desired amount of MB or TC sample solution was drawn and filtrated
through a 0.22 μm filter to determine the concentrations of MB or TC.
Additionally, the pH of all the above solutions were adjusted by
0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH and the sample tubes filled with TC solu-
tions were wrapped in aluminum foils to prevent exposure to light. The
amount of MB or TC adsorbed from the solution was calculated by
following equation (Eq. (1)):
qe=[(C0−Ce)V]/m (1)
where C0 and Ce (mg/L) are the initial and equilibrium concentrations,
V (L) and m (g) are the volume of the solution and the weight of the
BC/Ca-MMT used.
In last section, we conducted the co-adsorption experiment to in-
vestigate the performance of BC/Ca-MMT in the MB-TC binary system.
Typically, 10 mg BC/Ca-MMT was respectively introduced into a 20 mL
solution containing MB = 50 mg/L and TC = 50 mg/L, as well as a
20 mL simulated polluted water solution containing MB = 50 mg/L,
TC = 50 mg/L, humic acid (HA) =20 mg/L and sodium alginate
(SA) =20 mg/L. Both sets of mixtures were shaken at 30 °C for 5 h.
2.5. Regeneration of BC/Ca-MMT
To assess the reusability of BC/Ca-MMT, the samples were eluted
with ethanol solution for MB and TC desorption. The mixtures were
shaken for 5 h at 30 °C with a shaking speed of 150 rpm. The

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of BC/Ca-MMT
Morphological and microstructural details of BC, Ca-MMT and BC/
Ca-MMT have been illustrated in Fig. 1. The SEM images demonstrate a
well-organized 3D network structure of BC (Fig. 1a) and a layered
structure generated by stacked granules of Ca-MMT (Fig. 1b). The fine
nano-reticular microfibrillars of BC (10–20 nm in diameter) create an
expanded surface area and highly porous matrix that provide the op-
portunity for impregnation with submicron and nano sized Ca-MMT
particles (Meftahi et al., 2010). It was observed that in BC/Ca-MMT
(Fig. 1c) composites, the Ca-MMT particles attached tightly on each
fibril and no evidence of agglomerates indicating that the Ca-MMT were
homogeneously distributed throughout the BC matrix. In addition, the
retained nano-porous architecture of the composites is suitable for the
adsorption of various pollutants from aqueous solution. We then ana-
lyzed the surface elemental compositions of these samples by XPS. From
Fig. 2a, one could clearly see the appearance of Ca, Mg, Al, and Si peaks
in the composites, which does not exist in bare BC.
The chemical structure of BC, Ca-MMT and BC/Ca-MMT were
analyzed by FT-IR, which is an important tool to determine the func-
tional groups in a molecule (Fig. 2b). The peaks of BC, BC/Ca-MMT and
the broad band of Ca-MMT appeared at 3348 and 1640 cm

The thermal stabilities of BC and BC/Ca-MMT were compared by

hydrogen bond interactions between both inorganic and organic parts
causing an increase of the necessary force to disrupt the structure
(Darder, Colilla, & Ruiz-Hitzky, 2003).

3.2.1. Effect of pH studies
The pH values influence the charge transfer between liquid and
solid interface and play an important role in the adsorption process
(Jin, Xiang, Liu, Chen, & Lu, 2017). The results in Fig. 3b show that the
variation in trends between the adsorption of MB and TC are sig-
nificantly different. For MB adsorption, the adsorption capacity in-
creases with increasing pH and then almost no change at pH 8–10. This
may be due to there are abundant protons available at lower pH and
thus cause electropositivity in BC/Ca-MMT, which hinders the ad-
sorption of positively charged cationic MB dye. Meanwhile, there also
exists plenty of hydrogen ions (H
+
) compete with MB
+ 
for the free
active sites, which offsets the uptake of MB. While the solution becomes
alkaline, the strong electrostatic attractions between positively charged
cationic MB dye and negatively charged BC/Ca-MMT surface lead to an
increase in the MB removal. Additionally, the increasing deprotonated
silanol groups of Ca-MMT cause the number of negative charges on the
adsorbent surface increases (Liu, Omer, & Ouyang, 2018), which pro-
motes the reaction in this phase.
For TC adsorption, the adsorption capacity goes down with the rise
of pH values. As TC is an amphoteric molecule having multiple ioniz-
able functional groups, such as dimethylamino, phenolic deketone and
tricarbonyl methane groups (Liu et al., 2012). The three groups can
undergo protonation-deprotonation reactions according to the solution
pH. When pH below 3.3, TC exists in its cationic form (TC
+
) and the
adsorption mainly involves cation exchange with the negative charged
surface of BC/Ca-MMT. By increasing the pH from 3.3 to 7.68, it mainly
exists in the form of TC
0 
at acidic to neutral condition. The ability of
cation exchange gradually weakens, resulting in a gradual decrease of
the adsorbed amount. Then the further increased repulsion takes place
between deprotonated TC
− 
and BC/Ca-MMT in alkaline environment.
However, the adsorption capacity has not greatly decreased among pH
8-10. In this period, another mechanism behind the adsorption of TC
might be the formation of metal-TC complexes with Ca and Mg
(Sassman & Lee, 2005).

3.2.4. Co-adsorption studies
Here, MB-TC binary system was employed as the co-existed mixed
solutions system. Considering the acceptable difference in dye and
antibiotics removal, the co-adsorption experiments were conducted at
an original pH about 7.5. The addition of HA and SA were to simulate
the organic macromolecules such as humus and proteins in real water.
The photographs of the MB-TC mixture solutions before and after the
treatment of BC/Ca-MMT were depicted in Fig. 5a and b, along with the
qe values were presented in Fig. 5c. One sees the composites still exhibit
effective removal performance regardless the existence of HA and SA.
Interestingly, for 50 mg/L MB-TC solution, the adsorption amount of
MB or TC in natural condition (pH 7.5) complies with the removal
performance of the BC/Ca-MMT in single MB or TC adsorption in
Section 3.2.1 (Fig. 3b), indicating the specific removal of dye or anti-
biotic of our sample. In addition, after the adsorption of pollutants, we
could easily separate the adsorbent from the adsorption medium using
hand or tweezers, which was quite convenient for its further use. All
these suggest the BC/Ca-MMT sample equipped with certain practical
significance in actual sewage treatment.
3.3. Regeneration and reusability studies
The reusability of BC/Ca-MMT was examined through regeneration
and cyclic adsorption. The data in Table 1 show the qe value and re-
moval efficiency of BC/Ca-MMT samples in each cycle. One sees there
are very small decreases (both are less than 3%) of the adsorption ef-
ficiency in the fourth experiment. This phenomenon could be due to a
fraction of MB or TC molecules trapped in the intrapores of the sample
and thus caused incomplete desorption. In order to analyze the mor-
phology and chemical compositions of the regenerated samples, we
conducted SEM and XPS tests. As revealed in Fig. S2a, the regenerated
sample still maintained the original BC/Ca-MMT appearance and al-
most no change observable. Besides, the signals of characteristic ele-
ments of both pollutants were disappear after the sample regeneration
(Fig. S2b and S2c). The excellent reusability renders us to believe that
BC/Ca-MMT has great potential in decontamination of water.
4. Conclusions
In summary, this work reported a common organic-inorganic com-
posite material BC/Ca-MMT and its efficient removal performance of
cationic dyes and antibiotics. Electrostatic attraction, cation exchange
and hydrogen bonding interactions were the main mechanisms in-
volved in the adsorption process. The adsorption experiment results
showed the maximum adsorption amounts of BC/Ca-MMT were
338.8 mg/g for MB and 230.5 mg/g for TC under the condition we
examined. Meanwhile, the sample exhibited an effective and simulta-
neous removal of the two classes of pollutants in MB-TC binary system.
Furthermore, the separation of the sample could be simply achieved by
