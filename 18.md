1. Introduction
Since the discovery of penicillin in 1928, antibiotics have been
widely used to treat or prevent bacterial infections [1]. At present, more
than 100,000 tons are used per year worldwide, and the overuse and
misuse of antibiotics leads to their continuous release into the aquatic
environment via multiple pathways, increasing water pollution [2].
Tetracycline (TC), as one of the major antibiotics, has attracted

attention due to its high persistence and ecotoxicity that can affect
bacteria, which reduces its therapeutic potential against human and
animal pathogens [3]. Therefore, it is necessary to effectively and ef-
ficiently remove TC from aquatic environments. Various technologies
have been employed to remove TC from aqueous solutions, such as
membrane filtration [4–6], conventional activated sludge treatment
[7–9], adsorption [10,11], ultrasound [12–14], electrochemistry
[15,16], and advanced oxidation processes [17–19]. Among these, ad-
sorption has been widely researched due to its convenient and eco-
nomic advantages [20–22]. In adsorption, various adsorbents such as
biochar, iron oxide nanoparticles, multiwalled carbon nanotubes, gra-
phite, clay minerals, activated carbon, humic substances, and ion ex-
change resins have been used for the removal of TC [23–27].
Recently, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have increasingly at-
tracted attention due to their versatility and potential applications.
MOFS are metal ions/clusters and organic linkers with strong co-
ordination bonds with infinite and uniform crystalline coordination
networks [28–30]. MOF materials as a new advanced porous material
possess huge surface areas, tunable porosities and coordinatively un-
saturated sites, which have been reported to have been used for ad-
sorptive removal of various hazardous pollutants from water, including
dyes, pharmaceutical and personal care products (PPCPs), organic
pollutants (POPs), and spilled oil [31]. Seo et al. reported the ad-
sorptive removal of methylchlorophenoxypropionic acid (MCPP) from
water by MOFs, namely, Zr-benzenedicarboxylate (UiO-66). UiO-66
showed a very high adsorption rate, and its kinetic constant was ap-
proximately 30 times that of activated carbon because UiO-66 has a
smaller pore size than activated carbon [20]. Andrew Lin et al. prepared
magnetic graphene supported ZIF-67 as an adsorbent to remove ben-
zotriazole and found that ZIF–MG exhibited a high adsorption capacity
for benzotriazole, beyond 300 mg/g. Moreover, ZIF–MG could be re-
generated by ethanol-washing, and its regeneration efficiency remained
above 98 % after 4 cycles [32]. Zheng et al. prepared magnetic Fe3O4-
PSS@ZIF-67 (MZIF-67) composites with a core-shell that could effec-
tively adsorb methyl orange from water. Its maximum adsorption ca-
pacity was 738 mg/g. Therefore, the porous material MOFs show great
potential for adsorptive removal of pollutants from aqueous solutions.
However, it is necessary to recover the used MOFs from the water due
to the MOFS porous material being a powder nanomaterial, which may
have a potential impact on the water environment.
Graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets as a two-dimensional material
have attracted increasing attention due to their large specific surface
area and abundant functional groups, such as carboxyl hydroxyl and
epoxy groups, which have been regarded as the most effective ad-
sorbent. GO nanomaterial adsorbent also suffers from difficult solid-
liquid separation due to its ease of aggregation and release of powder,
resulting in secondary environmental pollution. It has been reported in
the literature that a macroscopic three-dimensional structure adsorbent
can support powder nanomaterials for convenient recycling after water
treatment and minimize any potential environmental pollution [33].
More importantly, their three-dimensional network structure can pre-
vent aggregation and ensure large-scale transmission to internal struc-
tures [34–36]. Alginate is an anionic polysaccharide extract consisting
of 1,4-β-D-mannuronic and 1,4-L-guluronic acid residues [37]. Alginate
has been used to support various compounds for water treatment due to
its nontoxicity, high biocompatibility, and relatively low cost. The al-
ginate surface functional groups such as carboxyl and hydroxyl can
cross-link with multivalent cations and form an insoluble “egg-box”
structure that will further produce a three-dimensional porous gel
network [38–41]. Zhu et al. reported a novel in situ growth approach
for incorporating metal–organic framework (MOF) materials into an
alginate substrate and demonstrated that the metal ion cross-linked
alginate hydrogels can be converted into MOF–alginate composites
through posttreatment of the hydrogels in MOF ligand solutions [42].
The “green” graphene-based hybrids with organic natural polymer
aligning seemed to be the available host materials for metal embedding
[43,44].
In this work, we report an easy preparation route for the in situ
growth of ZIF-67 crystals on an alginate-graphene substrate. The algi-
nate-graphene matrix with rich oxygen-containing functional groups
was formed into a hydrogel (AG-Co) through Co
2+ 
coordination, and
then the Co
2+ 
in the AG-Co acted as metal nucleation sites for the in
situ growth of ZIF-67 crystals to form alginate-graphene-ZIF67 (AG-
ZIF). Using the alginate-graphene as a substrate greatly overcame the
disadvantage of excessive growth, easy aggregation, and difficulty in
recycling the ZIF-67 crystal powder, and it also resulted in a smaller
size and highly dispersed ZIF-67 crystals on the substrate. In tetra-
cycline removal, the alginate-Co aerogel (A-Co), alginate-graphene-Co
aerogel (AG-Co), and alginate-ZIF aerogel (A-ZIF) were also used as
adsorbents to compare with AG-ZIF. The adsorption mechanisms were
deduced based on the adsorption isotherm, kinetic study and char-
acterizations.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
All chemicals, e.g. sodium alginate (molecular weight: 216.2), Co
(NO3)2·6H2O, KMnO4, H2SO4, H3PO4, H2O2, HCl, methanol, 1,2-
Dimethylimidazole were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent
Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Tetracycline was purchased from the
Chinese pharmacopoeia.
2.2. Preparation of alginate-GO-ZIF aerogel
Graphite oxide was obtained using the modified Hummers’ method
[45] (Supplementary data), 0.4 g of graphite oxide was dispersed in
100 mL of deionized water and ultrasonicated for 4 h to obtain the GO
solution. To prepare alginate-GO-Co hydrogel (named AG-Co), 4 g so-
dium alginate was dissolved in 100 mL deionized water and stirred for
4 h to obtain a homogeneous solution. Then, the 4 mg/mL GO solution
and the 40 mg/mL sodium alginate solution were mixed and stirred for
1 h to obtain a homogeneous solution(the final concentrations of GO
and sodium alginate are 2 mg/mL and 20 mg/mL, respectively.). The
mixed solutions were dropped into 0.10 mol L
−1 
Co(NO3)2·6H2O solu-
tion and incubated for 24 h to form the AG-Co hydrogel. Next, to pre-
pare the alginate-GO-ZIF hydrogel (named AG-ZIF), the prepared AG-
Co hydrogel was transferred into 200 ml of a 30 mg/mL 2-methylimi-
dazole in methanol solution and stirred for 4 h at room temperature.
For comparison, alginate-Co (A-Co) and alginate-ZIF (A-ZIF) hydrogels
were prepared by a similar process without GO, but the other condi-
tions were the same as above. Finally, all of the prepared hydrogels
were washed with ethanol and deionized water three times each and
subsequently freeze-dried for 24 h to obtain the AG-Co, AG-ZIF, A-Co,
and A-ZIF aerogels.
2.3. Batch adsorption experiments
Batch experiments were conducted to study the performance of TC
removal. Initially, Add 20 mg of A-Co, AG-Co, A-ZIF and AG-ZIF ad-
sorbents to a 40 mL sample vial, then add 20 mL of 100 mg/L tetra-
cycline solution, mix and place in a shaker at 150 rpm for 720 min at
room temperature. After the reaction, A-Co, AG-Co, A-ZIF and AG-ZIF
were separated by solid-liquid separation, and then the concentration of
tetracycline was measured at 360 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer
(Mapada, UV-6100). The isotherm studies were conducted at room
temperature and 150 rpm for 720 min with a solid to liquid ratio of
1 mg/mL and pH 6 under varying the initial tetracycline concentration
(50−500 mg/L). The kinetic studies were operated in 100 mg/L tetra-
cycline solution at room temperature and 150 rpm with a solid to liquid
ratio of 1 mg/mL and pH 6 under varying reaction time (5, 15, 30, 60,
90, 120, 180, 240, 360, 480, 720 min). The effects of pH were studied in

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Syntheses and material characterization of the adsorbent
3.1.1. Morphological characterization
As illustrated in Fig. S1, sodium alginate and GO were first co-
ordinated with Co
2+ 
ions to form the AG-Co hydrogel. The alginate
interacted ionically with carboxylic groups on different units of the
alginate chain through the Co
2+ 
ions, and through the oxygen func-
tional groups and carboxylate groups on the basal planes and edges, the
GO could bond to the Co
2+ 
ions. In addition, the alginate chain and GO
sheets are intertwined by strong hydrogen‐bonding interactions. Hence,
the Co
2+ 
ions were confined within the alginate in a special “egg box”
structure and between the GO sheet. Then, the alginate-GO-Co(AG-Co)
hydrogel was dispersed in the 2-methylimidazole methanol solution to
obtain an AG-ZIF hydrogel in which the 2-methylimidazole chelated
with Co
2+ 
ions on the alginate and GO matrix grow the ZIF particles
uniformly in situ. Finally, the alginate-GO-ZIF aerogel was obtained by
a simple freeze-drying process.
As shown in Fig.1, the surface morphology of the AG-ZIF aerogel
was observed using SEM analysis. It can be seen that the surface of the
AG-ZIF aerogel has a pleated structure and a rough bulge in Fig. 1a1.
The SEM results shown in Fig. 1a2 indicate that abundant and uniformly
distributed particles are anchored on the surface matrix of the alginate
and GO, which are irregularly polygonal-shaped crystals with a size of
approximately 250 nm and are suspected to be ZIF-67 particles. After
the in situ growth of the ZIF crystals in the alginate and graphene
frameworks, the size of the grown ZIF crystal particles was controlled
and aggregation of the ZIF crystals was prevented. In addition, the
sample still maintains a crosslinked three-dimensional aerogel structure
with wrinkles and defects. Thus, the wrinkles and defects in the surface
structure of the crystals provide more contact sites than the smooth
surface when reacting with pollutants. However, for the alginate-Co,
the surface structure is relatively smooth. On the surface of the alginate-
GO-Co, it can be seen that there are more wrinkles and pore structures,
but the surface of the pleats is smooth. By comparing the AG-ZIF and A-
ZIF aerogel, the A-ZIF can be seen to exhibit the typical morphology of a
three-dimensional structure of alginate aerogel with nonuniform crys-
tals on the surface. This indicates that the chelating of the alginic acid
chain and Co
2+ 
ions is not uniform, and the position of the crystals
generated in situ is also random.
To further understand the in situ growth of ZIF particles on the
surface of the AG-ZIF aerogel structural framework, the elemental
composition and spatial distribution of the AG-ZIF were studied by
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy analysis (EDS) mapping analysis
as shown in Fig. 1f. The relative atomic content of the elements in all

paring the AG-Co, A-ZIF, and AG-ZIF aerogels, it can be seen that with
the lower oxygen content, the Co
2+

aerogel after adding GO. It can be concluded that when GO is added,
the interactions between the Co
2+ 
ions and the alginic acid chain and
GO are increased, and the alginate chain also supports GO to prevent
aggregation, which plays a key role in the uniform in situ growth of
crystal formation on the rougher surface. This particular structure is

3.1.4. The BET surface areas and pore volumes analysis
ZIF particles generally have a large specific surface area, so ZIF
particles grown in situ on alginate and graphene may have an impact on
the specific surface area and pore structure of the aerogel. Fig.4 shows
the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm curves and the pore vo-

an adsorption hysteresis loop indicating the presence of mesopores,
which should be derived from a three-dimensional network structure
formed by sodium alginate and graphene aerogel. Compared with the
AG-Co, the BET surface area of AG-ZIF increases from 26.52 m

3.3. Reaction mechanism
To further understand the adsorption mechanism of TC on AG-ZIF,
FTIR analysis was used to study the surface of AG-ZIF after 6 cycles of
adsorption, as shown in Fig. S4. The main adsorption peak of the AG-
ZIF after the reaction is similar to that of AG-ZIF in which the intensities
of the individual adsorption peaks show great changes and there are
also slight changes in wavenumber, which may be caused by the in-
teraction between the AG-ZIF and the functional groups of TC. The
broader peak at 3194 cm
−1 
is due to the presence of −OH and a
broader CeO characteristic stretching vibration absorption peak at
1026 cm
−1
, indicating that an intermolecular hydrogen bond is formed
between AG-ZIF and the TC molecule. In addition, the peaks at
1567 cm
−1 
and 1602 cm
−1 
are due to the COO- stretching vibration
and C]N stretching vibration, respectively, while the peaks at 1567
cm
-1 
and 1602 cm
-1 
are obscured by a strong peak appearing at
1587 cm
-1
, indicating adsorption via the benzene ring of the TC mole-
cules. Since both AG-ZIF and TC have benzene rings in their molecular
structure, this further proves that the adsorption is through π-π inter-
actions and cation-π bonds. Then, through the in situ growth of the ZIF
crystals in the alginate and graphene frameworks, the AG-ZIF aerogel
has a large specific surface area and a rich pore structure to facilitate
adsorption of TC molecules (Fig. 8). Moreover, the wrinkles and defects
in the material provided more contact sites for TC. Due to the AG-ZIF
aerogel possessing negative charges at pH values from 2.0 to 11.0, there
are intermolecular forces between the AG-ZIG aerogel and the TC mo-
lecule. In addition, it can be seen from the kinetic data that the pseudo-
second-order kinetic model better describes the adsorption process. The
adsorption isotherm conforms to the Langmuir model, and the ad-
sorption process is mainly carried out by multimolecular layer ad-
sorption.

4. Conclusions
Preparation of MOF crystals into aerogel is beneficial for their ap-
plication in water treatment. Here, an alginate-graphene matrix with
rich oxygen-containing functional groups was formed into a hydrogel
(AG-Co) through Co
2+ 
coordination, and then the Co
2+ 
in AG-Co acted
as metal nucleation sites for the in situ growth of ZIF-67 crystals to form
alginate-graphene-ZIF67 (AG-ZIF). Using the alginate-graphene as a
substrate greatly overcame the disadvantages of excessive growth, easy
aggregation, and difficulty in recycling the ZIF-67 crystal powder, and
it also resulted in a smaller size and highly dispersed ZIF-67 crystals on
the substrate. The interaction between Co
2+ 
ions and the alginic acid
chain and GO became stronger, and the alginate chain also prevented
GO from aggregation. The BET surface areas of AG-Co and AG-ZIF
aerogels are 26.52 m
2
/g and 138.62 m
2
/g, respectively. In tetracycline
removal, the maximum adsorption capacities by AG-Co and AG-ZIF
were 105.49 and 456.62 mg/g, respectively. We deduced that the ad-
sorption site on the surface of the AG-ZIF was nonuniform with mul-
tilayer adsorption characteristics, which may mainly be due to different
adsorption sites including ZIF crystals and the oxygen-containing
functional groups on the alginic acid chain and GO in AG-ZIF. The
tetracycline adsorption on AG-ZIF was proven to involve π-π interac-
tions and cation-π bonds. After 6 cycles, the AG-ZIF aerogel still ex-
hibited a high adsorption capacity, which indicated its good reuse
ability. This study paves a new way for the preparation of polymer-
based MOF aerogels with great potential for water treatment.
