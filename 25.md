### Novel one step preparation of a 3D alginate based
### MOF hydrogel for water treatment

Growing efforts have been focused on processing MOF particles into hydrogels. We propose a novel
one-step method for the preparation of a novel 3D alginate based MOF hydrogel and use it for
tetracycline removal. Rather than the formation of ZIF-67-alginate hydrogel (AM-M) using Co-alginate as the
precursor, the Co
2+ 
chelates with alginate and 2-methylimidazolate simultaneously and forms into a different
MOF hydrogel (MA-M). The microstructure of the material turns from a smooth surface similar to the alginate
hydrogel into a uniform particle-like one, indicating the AM-M has a polymer skeleton while the MA-M has a
whole MOF structure. In tetracycline adsorption, the MA-M reaches equilibrium faster than AM-M. The
maximum adsorption capacity of tetracycline adsorption on MA-M is 364.89 mg g
1
, which is higher than that
of AM-M (302.32 mg g
1
). The MA-M has good regeneration properties after 10 cycles. The results pave a new
way for the preparation of novel polymer based MOF hydrogels with great potential for water treatment.
Introduction
Various pollutants are discharged into the environment and
cause serious water pollution problems around the world.
1
Knapp et al.
2 
found that from 1940 to 2008, all of the antibiotic
resistance genes obviously increased in five different areas of
the Netherlands. Among those antibiotics analyzed, tetra-
cycline resistance has increased by 15 times since the 1970s.
Although studies on antibiotics have never stopped, chronic
and acute effects of antibiotic-containing wastewater on ecology
have not yet been clearly understood.
3 
Therefore, it is very
necessary to develop effective techniques for antibiotic removal
from water.
At present, there are many methods commonly used for anti-
biotic removal including biodegradation,
4

5

6

7

For easier separation of the nanomaterial from water after
adsorption and inhibition of aggregation of the nanomaterial,
combining nanomaterials and polymers into composite hydro-
gels is shown to be useful.
23
Alginate is a natural polysaccharide extracted from seaweeds
which has a linear chain structure of (1–4)-linked b-D-mannuronic
acid (M) and a-L-guluronic acid (G) residues arranged in a block-
wise fashion. It has hydrophilicity, biocompatiblity, nontoxicity
and exceptional formability.
24 
Alginate chelates with divalent
cations to form hydrogels. Gel formation is driven by the
interactions between G-blocks which associate to form tightly
held junctions in the presence of divalent cations.
25 
Alginate
based hydrogels are usually used as adsorbents for water treatment
due to their great porous structures and mechanical properties.
26–30
Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), formed from self-assembly
of metal ions and organic bridging ligands, are novel porous
crystalline materials.
31 
Benefiting from ultrahigh porosity, control-
lable pore size, a crystalline nature, and highly ordered structures,
MOFs have been successfully applied in various areas.
32 
Growing
efforts have been focused on processing MOF particles into
spheres.
33 
Among them, incorporation of MOFs into three
dimensional polymer matrices is commonly used.
34 
Considering
that MOF–polymer composites prepared through simple blending
are easy to aggregate with poor particle–polymer matrix inter-
action, Zhu et al.
35 
prepared ZIF-67–alginate hydrogels using
Co-alginate hydrogels in a two-step method, which overcame
the challenges of processing MOF particles and was demon-
strated to be an effective absorbent for water purification.
However, the catalysis properties of this kind of material have
rarely been studied yet.
Therefore, in this study, we further simplify the preparation
routine and strengthen the interactions between MOF particles

Experimental section
Preparation of alginate-MOF hydrogels
The preparation routines for the alginate-MOF hydrogels MA-W,
MA-M, AM-W and AM-M are shown in Fig. 1.
MA-W: 2 g sodium alginate was dissolved into 100 mL distilled
water under room temperature conditions and stirred for 12 h to
get homogeneous sodium alginate solutions. Then, the sodium
alginate solutions were dropped into a 100 mL mixture solution
containing 40 mg mL
1 
Co(NO3)2 and 30 mg mL
1 
2-methylimid-
azolate, and then stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The
resulting alginate-MOF hydrogels named MA-W were washed
with ethanol twice and then dried at 60 1C.
MA-M: 2 g sodium alginate was dissolved into 100 mL distilled
water under room temperature conditions and stirred for 12 h to
get homogeneous sodium alginate solutions. Then, the sodium
alginate solutions were dropped into a 100 mL methanol solution
containing 40 mg mL
1 
Co(NO3)2 and 30 mg mL
1 
2-methylimid-
azolate, and then stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The
resulting alginate-MOF hydrogels named MA-M were washed
with ethanol twice and then dried at 60 1C.
AM-W: 2 g sodium alginate was dissolved into 100 mL distilled
water under room temperature conditions and stirred for 12 h to
get homogeneous sodium alginate solutions. Then, the sodium
alginate solutions were dropped into 100 mL 40 mg mL
1
Co(NO3)2 solutions and remained for 24 h to form Co-alginate
hydrogels. The prepared Co-alginate hydrogels were transferred
into 100 mL 30 mg mL
1 
2-methylimidazolate solution and
stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting alginate-MOF
hydrogels named AM-W were washed with ethanol twice and then
dried at 60 1C.
AM-M: 2 g sodium alginate was dissolved into 100 mL
distilled water under room temperature conditions and stirred
for 12 h to get homogeneous sodium alginate solutions. Then, the
sodium alginate solutions were dropped into 100 mL 40 mg mL
1
Co(NO3)2 solutions and remained for 24 h to form Co-alginate
hydrogels. The prepared Co-alginate hydrogels were transferred
into 100 mL methanol solution containing 30 mg mL
1 
2-methy-
limidazolate and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The
resulting alginate-MOF hydrogels named AM-M were washed
with ethanol twice and then dried at 60 1C.
Materials characterization
The surface morphologies of all the samples were characterized
using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL,
JSM-7001F). The surface functional groups were observed using
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker, Vertex 70).
The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were collected on a Bragg–
Brentano diffractometer (Rigaku, D/Max-2200) with monochromatic
CuKa radiation (l = 0.1542 nm). The specific surface area,
pore volume and pore size distribution were tested by nitrogen
adsorption/desorption at 77.4 K using an Accelerated Surface
Area and Porosimetry system (Micromeritics, ASAP 2020), and all the
samples were degassed at 373 K for 8 h before the measurements.
Tetracycline removal tests
Tetracycline removal was used for evaluating the pollutant
removal potential of the obtained hydrogels. The experiments
were conducted in a thermostatic shaker at 200 rpm for 12 h.
After reactions, the solid was separated from the solution using
a 0.45 mm membrane, and the tetracycline concentrations were
determined using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Mapada,
UV-6100) under 270 nm. For regeneration, the materials after
reactions were placed in distilled water and shaken for 24 h in
10 cycles. Each test contained a blank control and three replicates,
and the final equilibrium concentration was taken as the average of
the three replicates. The kinetic studies were operated under an
initial concentration of 300 mg mL
1 
in a thermostatic shaker in pH
8 at 298 K and 150 rpm under a solid to liquid ratio of 1 mg mL
1
.
The adsorption isotherms were studied in a thermostatic shaker in
pH 8 at 298 K and 150 rpm for 24 h under a solid to liquid ratio of
1 mg mL
1
. The initial concentrations of the antibiotics were 50,
100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 mg mL
1 
respectively. All samples were
measured in 3 duplicates and the averages were presented. After
adsorption, the samples were separated using a 0.45 mm membrane.
The residual concentrations in the solution were determined using
an ultraviolet spectrophotometer. Regeneration of the hydrogels was
carried out by putting the hydrogels after adsorption into distilled
water and shaking for 24 h.

However, for the products obtained from the one-step
method, MA-W and especially MA-M have characteristic structures
of MOFs which are totally different from polymer alginate
hydrogels. From the result we can see that the solvent plays
less of a role in the one-step method than the two-step method,
which is beneficial for green synthesis of MOFs to avoid the
use of toxic organic solvents such as methanol. MA-M is fully
composed of small particles in uniform shapes of 1 mm, and the
particle-like structure could provide more contact sites than the
smooth surface when reacting with pollutants. Though MA-W
and MA-M have a similar rough structure, the characteristics of
them are still different because the MA-W is ‘‘particles on winkles’’
while the MA-M is ‘‘flower-like particles’’. To further investigate the
structural differences of the hydrogels, EDS mapping of the MA-M
and AM-M is shown in Fig. 4. Element content percentages of the
hydrogels are shown in Table 1. MA-M contains more Co and less
Cl than AM-M, indicating that the MOFs have stronger interactions
with the alginate in the one-step method than the two-step one.
The high Co content in the material could provide more active
sites for pollutant adsorption. Moreover, the uniform element
distributions indicate the homogeneous structure of the novel
alginate-MOF hydrogel MA-M.
Structural analysis
To study the functional groups of the hydrogels, FTIR results
are shown in Fig. 5a. The broad band around 3400 cm
1 
is due to
the –OH stretching vibration.
37 
Peaks at 1616 cm
1 
are attributed
to the –COOH groups of alginate, while the peaks at 1407 cm
1
are due to the –OH formation vibration and the peaks at
1021 cm
1 
are due to the –C–O–C– stretching vibration.
38 
From
the results, it could be seen that the hydrogels from the one-step
preparation have obviously stronger –OH groups and –COOH
groups than those from the two-step preparation method. The
reason is that the interactions in the materials from the two
methods are different. In the two-step method, the alginate
chelates with Co
2+ 
first and then reacts with 2-methylimidazolate,
during which process the MOFs grow on the alginate matrix.
However, in the one-step method, Co
2+ 
and 2-methylimidazolate
react with alginate simultaneously, and the alginate turns into
MOFs directly. Therefore, the two-step method would lead to the
formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds while the one-step
method may lead to intramolecular hydrogen bonds, which make
the groups stronger in the novel 3D alginate based MOFs. In the
XRD results as shown in Fig. 5b, AM-M and AM-W do not have
obvious MOF bands because the polymer alginate is the main
component of the material. For the same reason, the peak for
MA-M is not very sharp. However, MA-M has the most obvious
peak among the four samples, indicating that the sample has
the best crystal structure, which further proves that the hydrogel
obtained from the one-step method may form into a new kind of
MOF-like material. Based on the results above, a scheme of the
material structures of MA-M and AM-M is shown in Fig. 6.

Conclusions
A novel one-step method for the preparation of a novel 3D
alginate based MOF hydrogel is proposed in this study. Unlike
the traditional polymer–MOF composite, the product obtained
in this study has an obviously different structure: the traditional
strategy is either mixing them together or growth of MOFs on the
polymer matrix, while in our study the polymer and the MOFs
become a new structure which is a wholly MOF-like structure
rather than the polymer-like one. In tetracycline adsorption, the
resulting novel MOF hydrogel is shown to have excellent
adsorption capacity and good reusability. The method presented
here is proved to be facile and versatile to induce the formation
of polymer based MOF hydrogels.
