Abstract: Detoxification of aflatoxin M1 from solution and milk using layered
double hydroxides was investigated. The Aluminum-Magnesium layered dou-
ble hydroxide (Al-Mg LDH) and Iron-Magnesium layered double hydroxide (Fe-
Mg LDH) were selected in their calcined and non-calcined forms to evaluate the
effect of the calcination on detoxification. These materials were produced using
the co-precipitation method. Preliminary adsorption tests confirmed use of Al-
Mg LDH as the selected adsorbent. Characteristics of the adopted adsorbent were
studied and confirmed by XRD, FTIR, SEM, and BET methods. Effects of the ini-
tial content of aflatoxin, amount of the adsorbents and detoxification time were
investigated. Influence of the adsorbents on the nutritional aspects of milk were
also studied. The study showed that while the non-calcined forms of LDH were
not able to adsorb aflatoxin M1 more than 23%, the calcined form of Al-Mg LDH
exhibited 100% adsorption in the solutions and about 70–100% in the contami-
nated milk samples. The reason is pointed to the fact that calcination of Al-Mg
LDH considerably increased the surface area, the total pore volume, and the pore
size of the material. Multivariate regression analysis and calculation of the Pear-
son correlation factor showed that the remained aflatoxin at each time was more
strongly correlated with the initial amount of aflatoxin and the elapsed time and
less strongly with the amount of the adsorbent. It was found that the adsorption
isotherms fitted to the Freundlich equation with a high adsorption capacity of
555.5 mg g−1.
K E Y W O R D S
Layered double hydroxide, aflatoxin M1, adsorption, Al-Mg LDH, Fe-Mg LDH, calcined
Practical Application: This study is focused on examining ability of layered
double hydroxides (LDH) for adsorbing AFM1. LDHs are promising layered
materials due to some of their interesting characteristics, such as ease of synthe-
sis and uniqueness of structure. In practice, results of this study can be used for
detoxification of aflatoxin, especially in milk, at high efficiency in shorter time
durations.

1 INTRODUCTION
Aflatoxins are a group of highly toxic compounds belong-
ing to mycotoxins that are categorized as secondary
metabolites of Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus parasiticus
and Aspergillus nomius. These are derivatives of difurans
coumarin with molecular weights of 315 to 346 Daltons
that not only negatively affect human and animal health
but also considerably influence the world
’
s economy and
trade. Among them B1, B2, G1, G2 and M1 are of more
importance. Consumption of aflatoxin B1-contaminated
feed by animals results in this aflatoxin being metabolized
as its 4-hydroxy derivatives called aflatoxin M1 (AFM1)
and excreted in milk and other liquids. The extent of
conversion of aflatoxin B1 present in animal feed to AFM1,
has been reported to be 1–4% while conversion ratios up
to 6% have been also observed (Daraei Garmakhany et al.
2010).
International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)
has categorized both of the aflatoxins B1 and M1 as group
1 of mutagens (World Health Organization, 2002). AFM1
is a confirmed carcinogen for the humans, which opposes
the immunity system and results in postponing of growth
in animals. Exposure to large quantities of this toxin can
result in vomitance and abdominal pain and even death
while lower quantities can produce liver cancer (Etzel
2002, Sherif et al. 2009). The allowable limit for AFM1 in
the European standard is 0.05 ppb (European Standards
2020). Based on the National Standard of Iran, AFM1 in
milk (pasteurized and non-pasteurized) is limited to 0.1ppb
and in dry milk for infant nutrition is 0.025 ppb (ISIRI
2011). Meanwhile, statistics of various countries by distri-
bution and extent of AFM1 contamination worldwide from
1988 to 2020 shows that prevalence of AFM1 in milk is at a
concerning level throughout the world (Salari et al. 2020).
While AFM1 in milk is relatively stable against most pro-
cesses as pasteurization and sterilization, it can also find
its way into the milk products. Various methods have been
suggested for elimination of aflatoxin from milk. Apart
from the methods introduced for reduction of aflatoxin
B1 (AFB1) in feed, several types of chemical, physical and
biological methods have a role to play in preventing and
reducing concentration of AFM1 in milk (Jalili and Scot-
ter 2015, Applebaum and Marth, 1982). Use of many of the
available physical and chemical procedures for the detoxi-
fication of milk contaminated with AFM1 is restricted due
to problems corresponding to safety issues, possible loss in
nutritional quality and limited efficacy and incurred costs.
This is while the adsorption procedure is categorized as a
practical solution for decontamination regarding the above
deficiencies. It has the dual benefit of reduction of afla-
toxin both in animal feed and in raw milk (Applebaum and
Marth, 1982).
The chemical and thermal stability of aflatoxins has
resulted in testing many approaches to decrease the afla-
toxin content in foods (Prandini et al. 2009, Carraro et al.
2014). The major part of the existing research works in
the field of adsorption is on animal nutrition and in vitro
aqueous experiments, but merely a few investigations have
concentrated on detoxification of milk contaminated with
AFM1 (Dixon et al. 2008, Jaynes and Zartman 2011).
In several studies where sequestering agents were evalu-
ated in vivo, their ability to protect animals from the effects
of dietary AFB1 and to eliminate or lighten AFM1 secretion
into milk were established.
Superior ability of physical adsorption utilizing differ-
ent bentonites against chemical methods has been inves-
tigated (Applebaum and Marth 1982, Soha et al. 2006, Car-
raro et al. 2014, Vila-Donat et al., 2018). Activated car-
bons and montmorillonite clays effectively bind AFB1 in
vitro (Galvano et al. 2001, Huwig 2001). Elimination of
aflatoxins from animal feed by bentonite has been exam-
ined (Phillips et al., 1995). Use of activated carbon, sodium
bentonite, esterified glucomannan, and activated charcoal
for reduction AFM1 in milk has been also successfully
examined (Nageswara and Chopra 2000, Diaz et al. 2003).
The binding affinity of aflatoxin to smectite minerals has
been identified to minimize the negative effects of afla-
toxin in food (Phillips et al. 2008, Di Natale et al. 2009) and
upgrade its safety for humans after ingestion (Wang et al.
2005, Afriyie-Gyawu et al. 2008b). Jaynes (2012) distin-
guished Cholestyramine, anion exchange resins, chitosan,
bentonite and layered double hydroxides to be potential
additives to bind fumonisins from water. Hsu (2018), stud-
ied fumonisin B1 (FB1) adsorption by Al-Mg LDHs and
found it to be primarily due to the electrostatic interactions
between the anionic part of FB1 and the positive charge
sites on LDHs. Matusik and Deng (2020) synthesized the
LDH materials by co-precipitation and examined their use
for FB1 removal. A higher adsorption capacity was attained
for the Al-Mg LDH in comparison to Fe-Mg LDH sam-
ples. However, it was admitted that fundamental research
on the use of LDH for mycotoxins removal was scarce and
incomplete.
As an important fact, adsorption is a surface character-
istic. The candidate material used for adsorption should
have high surface area, high porosity and maximum num-
ber of active sites. Layered double hydroxide (LDH) is rec-
ognized in this study to be the perfect material for adsorp-
tion as it has large surface area per unit mass as well as
large porosity. Therefore, LDH satisfies all the required
characteristics of adsorption. It has been applied mainly for
elimination of toxic metals, ions, organic dyes, and organic
materials from water and atmosphere (Chaara et al. 2010,
Yang et al. 2014, Pourfaraj et al. 2017, Peng et al. 2015), and
for water remediation from industrial pollution (Zubair

Layered double hydroxides are promising layered mate-

Considering the unique properties of LDH in material
absorbance, it is decided to study AFM1 adsorption by LDH
in the containing solutions as well as in the contaminated
milk. For this purpose, first two types of LDHs are prepared
using the co-precipitation method. Then, they are charac-
terized and evaluated for absorbance capacity, as detailed
in the next sections.
2 MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 Layered double hydroxides
preparation
Layered double hydroxides were synthesized by the co-
precipitation method at room temperature. While vig-
orously stirring, a solution containing 0.75 M of Mg
(NO3)2⋅6H2O and 0.25 M of FeCl3⋅6H2O or AlNO3.9H2O
was added at a rate of 1.0 mL/min to a solution of NaOH
(2 M) and Na2CO3 (0.2 M). The obtained slurry was treated
at 80
◦
C for 24 h and then centrifuged and washed with
deionized water until pH of the supernatant stabilized at
7.0. Afterwards, the acquired solids were dried at 80
◦
C for
24 h. Hereafter, the sample is called Fe-Mg LDH or Al-Mg
LDH.
2.2 Calcination of LDHs
The calcined types of Al-Mg LDH and Fe-Mg LDH were
attained by heating for three hours in a furnace at 500
◦
C.
Prior to analysis or use in adsorption experiments, both
the calcined and non-calcined products were ground and
passed through a 80-mesh sieve.
2.3 Preliminary adsorption experiments
For the purpose of selecting an appropriate LDH, Al-Mg
LDH and Fe-Mg LDH and their calcined counterparts were
considered in an initial test. In this experiment, 0.1 g of
the mentioned adsorbents was exposed to adsorb 1 ppb of
AFM1 at the times of 0, 10, 20, 30, 60, 120, and 180 minutes.
For the AFM1 adsorption process, the batch experiment
was utilized. Each adsorbent was added to the centrifuge
tubes containing solutions of AFM1. The dispersions were
shaken with constant agitation at the room temperature
in a vortex for the mentioned prescribed time durations.
After centrifugation, the supernatants were separated and
the amount of AFM1 was measured at each time by HPLC-
FLD as described in Sec. 2.9. As explained in the Results
section, the calcined Al-Mg LDH was finally selected as the
most effective and efficient adsorbent.
2.4 Al-Mg LDHs characterization
2.4.1 Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR)
To acquire the FT-IR spectra of Al-Mg LDH and its cal-
cined type, a Tensor 27 Bruker (Bruker Optics Ltd., Coven-
try, UK) was used in transmission mode from 400 to
4000 cm
−1

LDH and 100 mg of anhydrous KBr and carefully homog-
enizing the mixture in an agate mortar.
2.4.2 X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis
The dried Al-Mg LDH and its calcined form were inves-
tigated using X-ray diffraction by a Phillips/PANalytical
Xťpert Pro-MPD powder. The diffractometer was operated
at 40 kV and 40 mA using CuKα radiation source and SolX
energy dispersive detector with a step size of 0.05◦2θ and
a dwell time of 5 seconds per step at 1.54 angstrom.
2.4.3 Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
The morphologies of calcined Al-Mg LDH before and after
AFM1 adsorption were evaluated using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM). The samples were thoroughly dried,
coated by a thin gold layer (10 nm of thickness), and then,
captured with an electronic microscope (Philips XL 30,
Eindhoven, The Netherlands). The magnification of SEM
images was 125× and 500×.

3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Preliminary tests
In order to select an appropriate adsorbent, four different
adsorbents including calcined and non-calcined types of
Al-Mg LDH and Fe-Mg LDH were examined. The results
are exhibited in Figure 4. As observed, the non-calcined
variants of the mentioned adsorbents were not able to
adsorb and change the initial quantity of 1 ppb even up to
3 h. After lapsing 24 h, the RE was observed to be only 23%
in the Al-Mg LDH sample and merely 14% in Fe-Mg LDH.
These results clearly show that before calcination, the lay-
ered double hydroxide did not show the required ability to
adsorb aflatoxin even after an extended time of exposure.
For the calcined samples, the adsorption results showed
that the calcined Al-Mg LDH adsorbed 100% of 1ppb of
AFM1, being equivalent to 10 times the allowable amount,
in less than one hour of contact. This was while for the
calcined Fe-Mg LDH, the complete adsorption was only
possible in about 2 h. It was interesting that initially in

3.6 Discussion
Application of Al-Mg LDH and Fe-Mg LDH adsorbents
for reduction of aflatoxin from AFM1 contaminated solu-
tions and milk was investigated. In the course of study,
after assessing these two types of LDH before and after
calcination, it was observed that the non-calcined type
of LDH was not able to adsorb AFM1 more than 23% in
the Al-Mg LDH sample and 14% in Fe-Mg LDH. This is
while the calcined type proceeded in a rational time with
full adsorption of the toxin in the solution and 71–100%
in milk. This is a very important difference in adsorb-
ing capacity between the calcined and non-calcined types
of the adsorbents. In fact, the adsorption mechanism of
different toxic contaminants in LDH-containing hybrids
mainly depends on the type of hybridizing material and
pollutant. Generally, physical adsorption, hydroxide pre-
cipitation, anion-metal complexes, electrostatic interac-
tion, pi-pi interactions and chemical bonding are the com-
mon mechanisms involved in the adsorption process of
LDH containing hybrids (Zubair et al. 2017). According to
Faraji et al. (2021), possible interactions of LDHs include

For comparison of the Al-Mg LDH and Fe-Mg LDH, it

ability of adsorption in a considerably smaller time dura-
tion. This important finding agrees with the outcome of
the studies reviewed in the Introduction section of this
paper on absorbance of fumonisin (Jaynes 2012, Hsu 2018,
Matusik and Deng 2020).
As shown by Abdel Moaty et al. (Abdel Moaty et al.
2019), Fe-Mg LDH has a higher surface area than Al-Mg
LDH. However, the average pore diameter of Al-Mg LDH
is higher than Fe-Mg LDH. This fact can be the main rea-
son for the higher and faster rate of adsorption of AFM1 by
Al-Mg LDH. Presence of usually small amounts of AFM1
in milk can make more feasible the full adsorption by this
adsorbent.
Results of the surface area and pore size analysis pre-
sented in the current paper (see Sec. 3.2) revealed the
important fact that calcination of Al-Mg LDH approxi-
mately doubled the surface area of the material, increased
its total pore volume more than 54%, and increased its pore
size more than 35%. Increase of the mentioned character-
istics should be a clear clue why the calcined Al-Mg LDH
possesses much higher adsorption capacity than the non-
calcined Al-Mg LDH.
Color formation can be another reason for putting aside
use of Fe-Mg LDH for aflatoxin removal in milk. Pos-
sibly, change of the color of milk after addition of this
absorbent is because the Fe-Mg LDH accepts a dark color
after calcination that spreads into milk after addition of the
adsorbent.
Another significant feature of Al-Mg LDH is the fact that
no sensible reduction of the milk nutrients was detected
even after 24 hours of exposure to various amounts of the
adsorbent. Inclination for adsorbing none of the materials
including protein, lactose, and fat was witnessed.
As a final fact, it should be mentioned that distribu-
tion of AFM1 in milk is not homogeneous and a sig-
nificant amount of which binds to casein (Mohammadi
2011). This feature makes the adsorption mechanism more
complicated.
4 CONCLUSIONS
This study was focused on examining ability of LDH for
adsorbing AFM1. During the investigation, two types of
adsorbents consisting of Al-Mg LDH and Fe-Mg LDH
were studied in their non-calcined and calcined forms.
The XRD, FTIR, SEM, and BET methods were utilized
to confirm the characteristics of the adopted adsorbents.
The study illustrated that AFM1 adsorption was faster and
higher by the calcined forms of the LDH materials that was
related to the increase of the surface area, the total pore
volume, and the pore size of the material after calcination.
In addition, the adsorption process was much quicker by
use of Al-Mg LDH in place of Fe-Mg LDH. Statistical anal-
ysis of the data was performed and the Pearson correla-
tion factor was determined for the parameters influencing
the adsorption rate. Examination of the isothermal adsorp-
tion was also performed to identify the variation rule of the
adsorption capacity. The study findings can be itemized as
follows:
1. While in a short time of 10 min both of the adsor-
bents performed similarly, at longer times Al-Mg LDH
showed a faster rate of adsorption.
2. Through the Pearson and multivariate correlation anal-
yses, it was found that the remained AFM1 at each time
was more strongly correlated with the initial amount of
AFM1 and the elapsed time of exposure and less with
the amount of Al-Mg LDH.
3. Analysis of isothermal adsorption revealed that the
adsorption rate follows the Freundlich equation with a
high adsorption capacity of 555.5 mg g−1.
4. Regarding variation of the adsorption rate with quantity
of the adsorbent, it was observed that use of 0.01 to 1g
of the adsorbent increased the rate of adsorption from
70% to 100%.
5. Level of adsorption of AFM1 from milk varied between
70 and 100%. In comparison to the solution, adsorption
of AFM1 from the milk matrix happened in longer times
at lower rates such that at equal times, the adsorption
rate differed from 70 to 100%.
6. In any case, adsorption of higher amounts of aflatoxin
required longer times and larger quantities of the adsor-
bent.
7. Amounts of the milk nutrients including fat, protein,
lactose, and the dry matter were evaluated after the milk
samples were exposed to the calcined Al-Mg LDH for 24
hours and none of them was found to change consider-
ably, as the maximum variation was less than 5%.
In summation, this study clearly identifies Al-Mg LDH
as a very effective alternative for adsorption of AFM1 from
milk. More important, it paves the way for examining other
various LDH compounds for adsorption of different afla-
toxin varieties from food products. Obviously, much more
research is needed in this regard.
