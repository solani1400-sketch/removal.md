This work deals with the aflatoxins M1 detoxification of bovine milk by adsorption processes. Several
types of natural and industrial sorbents have been analyzed by considering their effects on the quality
of the treated milk and their removal efficiencies.
Treated milk characterization tests in terms of organic acids, lactose, chlorides, pH and protein contents
show a moderate alteration of the milk properties proportionally with the sorbent dosage. On the other
hand, experimental evidences reveal that the highest removal efficiencies (g > 90% for AFMs = 0.5 lg/kg)
have been obtained for the activated carbons due to the concurrent effects of high surface area, suffi-
ciently wide micropore size and higher affinity between the AFM molecules and the aromatic structure
of the carbons. Also the bentonite shows significant removal efficiency, combined with lower side effects
on the treated milk.
Therefore, this preliminary analysis shows that an optimization of sorbent dosage should be considered
to account for both a reliable detoxification of the milk and for assuring the acceptability of the treated
milk in light of its direct reuse for human consumption or as raw material for dairy products.

1. Introduction
Mycotoxins are secondary metabolites of some organisms of the
fungus family and they contaminate a wide range of crop plants
and fruits before or after harvest. As suggested by their own name,
which literally means ‘‘fungus poisons”, some of them are highly
toxic, causing severe diseases and sometimes the death both in hu-
mans and in farm animals (Hatch et al., 1982).
Mycotoxins may reach humans by direct ingestion of contami-
nated crops, by eating foods (oil, wine, peanut butter, nut paste,
etc) deriving from contaminated commodities, and by consump-
tion of meat and dairy products coming from infected animals. In
fact, mycotoxins present elevated chemical stability and they resist
to decomposition during typical industrial and household food
preparation treatments. Furthermore, they resist to animal diges-
tion processes and they are partially accumulated in tissues and
excreted with milk after metabolization.
According to the FAO more than 25% of the world agricultural
production is contaminated by mycotoxins. This result in economic
losses estimated at $923 million annually in the US grain industry
alone. Similarly, the large world consumption of foods as milk (619
Mtonne/year in 2004), oil (around 88 Mtonne/year in 2000) and
wine (277 Mhl/year in 2005), makes the presence of mycotoxins
in liquid products a severe problem for alimentary industry.
Most countries have adopted severe regulations to limit the
exposure to mycotoxins, having strong impact on food and animal
crop trade. The presence of mycotoxins is unavoidable. Therefore,
testing of raw materials and products is required to keep our food
and feed safe.
Aflatoxins are among the most dangerous mycotoxins. They are
present in several crops as a result of their contamination by
Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus ascomicetic fungi.
There are 18 different types of mycotoxins, among which the
most diffuse are the B1, B2, G1, G2, M1 and M2.
The last two compounds derive from the metabolization of B1
and B2 that occurs in the liver of animals due to the consumption
of contaminated foods. They are accumulated in the tissues and, if
possible, they are excreted through milks (Ellis et al., 1991; Ismail
and Rustom, 1997; Yiannikouris and Jouny, 2002). The chemical
structures of M1 and M2 aflatoxins (named in the following
AFM1 and AFM2 or, all together, as AFMs) are reported in Fig. 1.
All the aflatoxins are highly stable to thermal treatment (they
are thermally degraded only above 240 °C) and have a slightly acid
character. The presence of AFMs in milks leads to the contamina-
tion of dairy products, as they are not eliminated by the typical
processes of food industries as well as by food cooking. Ottaviani
(1991) showed that AFMs are mainly present in the milk serum
(46.5%) and in the casein (48.5%) while only a minor portion
is contained in the fat fraction (5%).
European Union has defined the maximum level of AFM1 and
AFM2 in milk by means of the CE 2174/2003 Regulation. For
the case of milks for human consumption, the highest allowed

Techniques to reduce aflatoxins concentration in liquid foods
include prevention strategies to reduce the fungal contamination
before harvest, decontamination methods to select only the uncon-
taminated commodities and detoxification procedures aiming to
deplete the mycotoxin content of foods by means of physical,
chemical or biological treatments.
The main drawback of decontamination processes is related to
the intrinsic complexity of recognizing and separating the contam-
inated crops from the uncontaminated ones. Nowadays, there are
no automatic methods for the rapid detection and separation of
aflatoxins–contaminating crops. Indeed, while for small-scale
productions a manual selection can be reasonably adopted, for
large-scale productions the manual selection becomes almost
inapplicable. For this reason, the detoxification of contaminated li-
quid foods appears to be much more reliable than the decontami-
nation of the original raw materials.
Detoxification treatments (e.g. Piva et al., 1995) should be tech-
nically and economically reliable, and should meet the criteria
listed by the FAO/WHO/UNEP Conference on Mycotoxins held in
Nairobi, Kenya in 1997. According to these criteria the ideal pro-
cess: (a) destroys or inactivates the toxin, (b) does not produce
toxic or carcinogenic products in the finished product, (c) destroys
fungal spores and mycelia that could proliferate and produce the
toxin, (d) preserves the nutritive value and acceptability of the
product, and (e) does not significantly alter important technologi-
cal properties of the product.
In line of principle, the adsorption treatments seem to intrinsi-
cally achieve some of these criteria as they are usually cost effec-
tive, they exclude the formation of secondary contaminants and
they are proven to have high removal efficiency for AFB–AFG re-
moval from aqueous and organic solutions. Furthermore, a peculiar
feature of adsorption processes is their ability to auto-adapt to in-
flow pollutant content. This feature is of the greatest relevance for
the high variability of aflatoxin concentrations in natural contam-
inated milks.
Variety of adsorbent materials like activated carbons and clays
has been shown to capture B and G aflatoxins in aqueous solutions
(e.g. Galvano et al., 1995; Dakovic´ et al., 2000, 2005; Diaz et al.,
2002; Phillips et al., 1988). More details on the experimental
conditions and the obtained results are reported in Table 1, show-
ing that adsorption treatment usually allows a very high removal
of aflatoxins from water. Furthermore, activated carbons and
phosphosilicate clay are currently used as alimentary integrator
for grazing animals as they bind aflatoxins in their intestinal appa-
ratus reducing the occurrence of pathologies and contamination of
milk (Hatch et al., 1982; Galvano et al., 1996; Nageswara Rao and
Chopra, 2001; Diaz et al., 2004).
An early study by Applebaum and Marth (1982) pointed out
that the adsorption on bentonite of AFMs from naturally contami-
nated milk allows a removal efficiency ranging from 65% to 89% by
increasing solid loading from 5 to 20 g/kg. Batch experimental tests
have been carried out at 25 °C and shows that the protein content
of the treated milk is around the 95% of the original material.
This study aims to verify the reliability of adsorption process as
a method for milk detoxification by experimental comparison of
several conventional and unconventional sorbents. Lab-scale tests
consist in the screening of the sorbent removal efficiencies for dif-
ferent kinds of activated carbons, zeolites, clinoptilolite, bentonite
and char and are carried out on bovine milk artificially contami-
nated with AFM1 standard solutions. The treated milk is eventually
analyzed in order to test the sorbents effects on its nutritional
properties.
2. Experimental analysis
2.1. Materials
2.1.1. Milk
The sample milk is obtained from an UHT whole milk produced
in the northern area of the Caserta province, in Italy, artificially
contaminated with standard AFM1 standard solution (Supelco re-
agent grade 46319-U) to obtain a mycotoxin content of 0.5 lg/
kg. Standard solutions are former dilute 1:100 in methanol and
then added to the milk sample.
2.1.2. Adsorbents
Adsorption equilibrium tests have been carried out with nine
different sorbents, four carbonaceous materials, three commercial
zeolites and two minerals. As regards the carbons, experiments
are carried out with three type of activated carbons: Filtrasorb
400 (Calgon Carbon), Aquacarb 207EA (Sutcliffe Carbon) and
GCN1240 (Norit). These are non-impregnated granular activated
carbons commonly used for water treatments. They are character-
ized by a high specific area, mainly related to their microporosity,

2.2. Methods
Adsorption tests have been carried out in batch reactors con-
taining the AFMs contaminated milk and a given amount of the
sorbent. The tests are repeated in triplicate.
A fixed volume of the contaminated milk (200 ml) is stored in
250 ml PE bottles and is contacted with a sorbent dosage ranging
between 2.5 and 50 g/kg. The sample is kept at +4 °C in a PID con-
trolled oven under gentle mechanical agitation. After equilibration,
the sorbent is separated by sieving with a 200 US-mesh sieve and
the milk is analyzed to determine the residual AFM1 content.
The analytical method (Dragacci and Grosso, 2001) is based on
the cleanup of the sample by an immunoaffinity column and the
determination of AFM1 by liquid chromatography with a fluores-
cence detector. The lower limit for AFM1 concentration is around
0.002 lg/kg.

3. Results
3.1. Effect of sorbent properties
The adsorption of AFM1 depends on the superficial properties of
the sorbents either in terms of surface morphology (i.e. surface
area and porosity structure) and properties of superficial chemical
groups. As a general rule the adsorption capacity of a given sorbent
toward a specific substance increases by increasing the surface
area, by assuring that the pores are sufficiently large to allow the
access of the given substances and by the assessment of stronger
chemical affinities between the sorbent superficial groups and
the adsorbed substance (Alfarra et al., 2004).
Accordingly, Table 2 resumes the adsorption efficiencies, g, of

ents of the used sorbents.
Experimental results show that the highest removal efficiencies
are obtained for the activated carbons (above 90%) and the benton-

Furthermore, the adsorption capacity increases with the surface

These results confirm that the most useful way to remove AFM1
from milk relies on the diffuse superficial interactions between the
aromatic structure of the carbons and the polyaromatic AFM1
molecule.
The distribution of AFM1s in the different components of milk
gives further explanation of this evidence. Ottaviani (1991)
showed that AFMs are mainly present in the milk serum and in
the casein. Serum is mainly represented by water and the AFMs
hydrophobicity promotes the capture on the more affine carbon
surface. On the other hand, casein represents almost the 3% by
weight of the total milk and contains around the half of the total
AFMs. Since casein is formed by large micelles (10–300 nm), it
can be assumed that the sorbent microporosity promotes the cap-
ture of the smaller AFM1 molecules rather than that of the larger
casein molecules.
Finally, by comparison with previous investigations (e.g.
Galvano et al., 1995; Dakovic´ et al., 2000, 2003, 2005; Diaz et al.,
2002; Phillips et al., 1988) the experiments confirms that the re-
moval efficiency of AFM1 in milk are lower than that observed
for AFBs and AFGs in aqueous solutions (Table 1). This result can
be easily explained by considering the different solubility/hydro-
phobicity of AFs in water and milk samples. Indeed, this effect is
much more pronounced for the clinoptilolite and the bentonite
rather than for the activated carbons, which confirm their high
selectivity towards aflatoxins.
3.2. Effect of sorbent loading
The effect of sorbent loading on AFM1 removal efficiency is ana-
lyzed for the three activated carbons and the bentonite (Fig. 3).
Experimental results show that the removal efficiency progres-
sively increases with sorbent loading for all the sorbents. Anyway,
while for the case of bentonite, an almost asymptotic value is
reached for a sorbent loading around 25 g/kg, for the activated car-
bons it can be observed a slower but progressive increase of the
adsorption efficiency. Experiments show that the activated carbons
accomplish the regulation limits (clim = 0.05 lg/kg) with a sorbent
loading lower than 50 g/kg for AFM1 concentration as high as ten
times the clim. On the contrary, bentonite is not able to obtain
the desired value of AFM1 concentration. Anyway, it has to be no-
ticed that lower AFM content can be eventually treated with lower
sorbent dosage.

The treated milk is analyzed in order to assess the effect of the
sorbent addition on its chemical properties. In particular, the
experiments consider the pH, the most abundant organic acids
(lactic, citric and maleic), chlorides, lactose and total protein
concentration.

For all the sorbents, the adsorption treatment reduces the con-
tent of the organic acids, chlorides, lactose and total protein and in-
creases the milk pH. The stronger effects are visible for the Norit
GCN1240 and the Filtrasorb 400 activated carbons. Indeed, for a
sorbent dosage of 35 g/kg only the lactose content is decreased be-
low the reference values (Table 3) while at 50 g/kg also the protein
and the chloride contents result lower than the limit values.
On the contrary, the organic acids are more strongly reduced by
the char and the bentonite at both the sorbent loadings.
Finally, the milk pH tends to be above the usual reference values
for all the sorbents and at both the loading levels. While the reduc-
tion of the above mentioned substances is related to parallel
adsorption phenomena, the alteration of solution pH is mainly re-
lated to the surface hydrolysis of the sorbent whose dynamic is fas-
ter than that of the adsorption (e.g. Yacoumi and Tien, 1995;
Benjamin, 2002; Di Natale et al., 2008).
It is known, however, that this effect is much more evident in
batch reactors, like those considered here, than in fixed bed col-
umns. In fact, while in the batch reactors the sorbent tend to alter
the pH of all the liquid phase, the dynamic of fixed bed columns
lead to the occurrence of a time vs pH profile: initially, when the
first liquid enters the columns, the sorbent rapidly tend to hydro-
lyze taking the outflow solution at a pH level closer to its point-of-
zero-charge pH (Noh and Schwarz, 1990). After a while, the sor-
bent equilibrates with the flowing liquid and the column outflow
maintains the original pH value of the inflow.
4. Conclusions
This paper reports experimental results on aflatoxins detoxifica-
tion of bovine milk by means of adsorption treatment. The reliabil-
ity of the process is considered both in terms of removal efficiency
and in terms of interferences with the quality of the treated milk.
Experimental tests show that the activated carbons have by far
the highest removal efficiency (g > 93% for AFMs concentration as
high as 0.5 lg/kg), followed by bentonite (g  80% at 0.5 lg/kg).
The correlation between sorbent properties and adsorption effi-
ciencies shows that AFM adsorption is relevant only if the average
size of sorbent micropores is higher than a critical value (10 Å)
and it tends to increase with the B.E.T. surface and with the pore
size.
The capture of AFM mainly depends upon low energy van der
Waals interactions involving the sorbent surface rather than spe-
cific superficial functional groups. As a consequence, the higher
adsorption capacity shown by the activated carbons appears to
be related to the contemporary effects of high surface area, suffi-
ciently wide micropore size and higher affinity between the AFM
molecules and the polyaromatic structure of the carbons. The
slightly lower removal efficiency of the bentonite reflects, on one
hand, the lower pore size and B.E.T. area of this sorbent but it also
suggests that the surface–AFMs affinity should be comparable with
the case of activated carbons.
Finally, the adsorption treatments tend to reduce the concentra-
tion of nutritional factors proportionally to the sorbent loading
with more pronounced effects for activated carbons. Indeed, with
the only exception of Norit GCN1240, a sorbent loading as high
as 35 g/kg preserves the levels of proteins, chlorides, and organic
acids concentrations within acceptance limits. This sorbent con-
centration allows, for both the activated carbons and the bentonite,
removal efficiency higher than 80%. The highest sorbent loading
(50 g/kg) assures higher efficiencies but it also results in larger ef-
fects on proteins and chlorides for the activated carbons, and on

These results represent a possible limit to the use of adsorption
process since it may not allow the direct reuse of treated milk for
human consumption. Indeed, two considerations are required. First
of all, it is worth noticing that lower sorbent loading (and then
lower modification of the milk properties) can be used for milk
contaminations of AFMs below 0.5 lg/kg. Furthermore, whether
the treated milk should not be reused directly, it can be used as
base material for the preparation of malted flour or dairy products.
This may be a significant result since, for instance, the actual policy
of Italian milk industry is to consider the contaminated milk as a
disposable waste material.
In conclusion, this preliminary study points out that the adsorp-
tion is a promising, reliable, method to remove aflatoxins from
milk whose application should be optimized in order to assure
both the required detoxification and the preservation of milk prop-
erties within given acceptability limits that allow its reuse either as
a food or as raw material for dairy products. Further experimental
and pilot plant investigations should be hoped for in the future to
verify the industrial reliability of this treatment.
